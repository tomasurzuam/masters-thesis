Meritocratic beliefs

When discussing education, it is difficult not to encounter the concept of meritocracy. Based on Michael Young’s work The Rise of Meritocracy (1962) (2017), this concept is understood as the distribution of rewards based on effort and talent. For their part, schools operate by awarding credentials that reflect students’ performance in various subjects. In this sense, meritocracy is a fundamental element of every educational institution, as it is one of the principles governing the awarding of credentials. Thus, beliefs about the educational system are often closely tied to meritocracy (Clycq, Ward Nouwen, y Vandenbroucke 2014; Darnon, Smeding, y Redersdorff 2018).

In the field of education, meritocracy has been used as a distributive principle, but within this framework it has also been linked to the justification of inequalities. Starting from Adams’ theory of equity (1963), meritocracy operates as a universal equality principle, where rewards should be commensurate with effort. Several studies have shown that meritocratic beliefs are associated with individualistic beliefs such as an aversion to redistribution and a preference for market justice (Castillo et al. 2024; Castillo, Iturra, y Carrasco 2025; Mijs 2021; Sin et al. 2026; Bartram 2023), and are a characteristic value of those in more privileged social positions (Heuer et al. 2020). In these cases, meritocracy is used as a means of legitimizing positions, where those who enjoy the benefits of the system tend to justify their success on the basis of their own merit, overlooking the structural conditions that shape reality. Thus, the narrative of merit is used to support the idea that money should determine the quality of and access to education. This normative judgment runs counter to educational justice, as it hinders equal opportunities and, ultimately, determines students’ educational trajectories (Moyano 2020; Walzer 2010).

In the meritocracy research framework, most studies have been based on survey items where International Social Survey Program emerges as the main instrument to measure and analise meritocratic beliefs both nacional and internacional contexts. This items show the relevance of meritocratic and priviliege factors for getting ahead in life like the effort and the contacts respectively. Following this conceptual model, there is a tradition of studying meritocratic beliefs from a unidimensional perspective (García-Sánchez et al. 2019; Mijs 2021). The fundamental assumption of this perspective is that the factors are negatively correlated; for example, if one expresses a higher degree of meritocratic beliefs, consequently, beliefs that account for structural factors decrease. The problem with this approach is that it has serious implications for understanding social reality, since people’s beliefs cannot be assumed to follow a rational logic where, if one goes up, the other must go down. To address this, in recent years several authors have proposed a multidimensional perspective on meritocracy, constructing frameworks that allow for distinguishing the dimension of merit from that of privilege, on the one hand, and differentiating perceptions from preferences on the other (Castillo et al. 2023; Duru-Bellat y Tenret 2012; Zhu 2025). These advances have led to two key contributions: to capture the coexistence of beliefs that may be considered contradictory to reflect the coexistence of beliefs that may be considered contradictory (1) and, consequently, to demonstrate that people can hold different sets of meritocratic beliefs (2).

While the multidimensional perspective of meritocracy allows for a more detailed examination of people’s beliefs, a caveat remains regarding the individuals who hold those beliefs. The literature has shown a prevalence of variable-centered methods—that is, methods that identify an association between two or more variables in a general population, assuming that such an association is uniform across individuals (Cech y Blair-Loy 2010). Following this approach, it is not possible to determine whether there are different configurations of people’s beliefs and what factors influence this differentiation. Given these limitations, latent class analysis (LCA) is a novel, person-centered method that allows us to identify the heterogeneity of individuals’ beliefs based on their clustering. One of the advantages of this approach is that it accommodates the possibility that individuals may hold both meritocratic and structuralist beliefs, allowing for a theoretical and methodological examination of multidimensional meritocracy (Zhu 2025; Nylund-Gibson y Choi 2018). By identifying latent groups, LCA enables the observation of qualitative typological variations, which in turn facilitates a detailed examination of how contextual variables interact with individual variables to shape people’s meritocratic beliefs.

Although LCA is not a widely used method in sociological studies, some research has demonstrated its effectiveness in identifying heterogeneous groups and the variations that persist across countries (Van Hootegem 2022). Zhu (2025) presented how meritocratic beliefs vary in the United States, Finland, and China, where three types of latent groups were identified: those with purely meritocratic beliefs, predominantly meritocratic beliefs, and dual consciousness. Therefore, based on previous studies, this research hypothesizes three types of groups based on meritocratic beliefs:

\(H_{1}\): (1) purely meritocratic beliefs, viewing merits as important while deeming structural factors as irrelevant; (2) purely structuralist beliefs, viewing structural factors as important while deeming merits as unimportant; (3) dual consciousness, viewing merits and structural factors as important simultaneously.
