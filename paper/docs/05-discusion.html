6  Discussion

Previous research has found a positive relationship between meritocratic beliefs and market justice in education. However, the purpose of this study is, first and foremost, to demonstrate that meritocratic beliefs must be examined alongside beliefs about privilege, addressing both individual and structural explanations that may account for success. More specifically, the main objective is to demonstrate that there are groups with different configurations of meritocratic and privilege beliefs, which have divergent relationships with market justice in education. Therefore, a person-centered method was applied to construct typologies based on merit and privilege, and then to study their relationship with market justice in education.

The study’s results identified three latent profiles that combined perceptions and preferences regarding merit and privilege: a profile that combined strong preferences for meritocracy and weak preferences for privilege; a profile that showed similar preferences for both meritocratic and privilege factors; and, finally, a profile that perceived privilege to a lesser extent than the previous two, combined with moderate preferences for both meritocracy and privilege. The first two profiles described can be partially aligned with those found by Zhu (2025), supporting one latent group characterized by a prevailing adherence to meritocracy and another with dual awareness, which recognizes the role of both individual and structural conditions. However, the findings of this research show that these profiles differ fundamentally in their preferences regarding privilege; while the meritocratic profile rejects them, the other embraces them. This reflects that support for or rejection of privilege is a fundamental characteristic for elucidating various patterns of belief. On the other hand, the third profile cannot be linked to the same background factors. One possible interpretation for this group is that these are individuals with a naive attitude toward how society functions (as they have the lowest perception of privilege), in addition to maintaining a passive attitude toward social reality, where moderate preferences seem to be an idea internalized by the system rather than a value that drives these individuals’ normative judgments.

By focusing on the relationship between latent profiles and market justice in education, we find something new: the configurations of meritocratic beliefs and privilege embodied by the profiles determine the legitimization of educational inequalities in different ways. Figure 5.8 already suggests a distinct relationship between the “Congruent Meritocrats” and the other two profiles, while the regression model reconfirms this. This finding is key, as it challenges the positive relationship between meritocracy and market preferences—a relationship supported in the literature by hypotheses of self-interest or the adoption of the meritocratic narrative as a justification for social status (Bartram, 2023; Castillo et al., 2024; Castillo, Laffert, et al., 2025; Castillo et al., 2026; Heuer et al., 2020). Here, the existence of “Congruent Meritocrats” demonstrates that a strong belief in meritocracy does not necessarily lead to a greater preference for market-based criteria. In this case, the pattern of beliefs that defines the profile helps explain the reason for this relationship: because they perceive that privilege is rewarded in society at the expense of merit, they prefer merit as the primary factor in the distribution of rewards. This aversion to privilege is ultimately consistent with opposing the idea that the ability to pay should be a determining factor in accessing a good education. The other two profiles offer a different explanation: the coexistence of merit and privilege in its ascriptive dimension leads to support for the idea that basic socioeconomic conditions should determine the education people receive. Thus, this dual perspective held by privilege advocates does not imply a challenge to structural conditions, but rather acknowledges—and even endorses—that privileges should function as a distributive criterion at the societal and educational levels. This may be related to studies such as those by Khan & Jerolmack (2013) and Gogescu (2024), which suggest that the recognition of merit coexists with the protection of privileges. Another explanation can be found in the ideas put forward by Scharff (2021), who notes that in a context of high inequality such as Chile’s, some people view this reality as inevitable and would therefore prefer to use any resource at their disposal to advance their careers. Thus, by examining meritocracy alongside privilege, we can highlight that the meritocratic narrative can legitimize inequalities, but it can also serve as a mechanism to mitigate educational disparities.

Education was found to have an effect opposite to that posited in \(H_{1}\); therefore, there is no evidence in support of this hypothesis. The data indicate that a higher level of education is negatively associated with market justice in education, demonstrating that educational training fosters a perspective capable of recognizing the role that structural conditions play in the access to and quality of social services—such as education—and opposes this (Busemeyer, 2012; Freire, 2015). This perspective is associated with what the literature has termed the “enlightening effect” of education. While this finding runs counter to the posits of authors such as Nye et al. (2026) or Van Noord et al. (2019), it is important to consider the regional and national context. In a region such as Latin America—historically marked by conflicts that repeatedly bring inequality and the influence of socioeconomic conditions on life trajectories to the forefront—education may have a more decisive illuminating effect than in regions with greater well-being (Dussel, 2019). In contrast, income did not show a significant effect in any of its quintiles; therefore, there is insufficient evidence to support \(H_{2}\). Furthermore, a Bottom/Middle/Top operationalization (40/50/10) was tested, but no effects were found there either.

The relationship between political identification and market justice in education followed the expected pattern, providing evidence to support \(H_{3}\). Here, identification with the left aligns with the progressive tendencies that have typically characterized this group on various issues, such as reducing inequality, expanding social rights, and improving quality of life, for instance (Rivera, 2023). In contrast, groups that identify with the right in terms of political ideology tend to support market-based distribution criteria (Castillo, Iturra, et al., 2025; Lee & Stacey, 2024), and are less likely to align with criteria of equity and equality (Van Hootegem, 2022). Although this pattern of relationships is consistent with what has been previously found in the literature, a specific finding emerged when the interactions between the different latent profiles and political identification were examined: Congruent meritocrats who identify with the right exhibit a moderation in their rejection of market-based justice in education. This can be explained by the identity realignment currently taking place on the right; in the past, the right found a coherent discourse within a defined value system, whereas now some right-wing groups have distanced themselves from the mainstream, which is reflected in the fact that adherence to this political spectrum can coexist with supposedly contradictory conditions (Häusermann et al., 2026). Thus, within a latent group that rejects privilege, identification with the right acts as a catalyst for the legitimization of inequalities.

In summary, the results show that studying belief patterns using person-centered methods can provide new insights into how perceptions and preferences regarding merit and privilege are organized in people’s minds. Beyond that, this heterogeneity in people’s attitudinal makeup reveals that, by studying merit alongside privilege, we can observe that the relationship between merit and the legitimization of inequalities is not so straightforward. In this context, depending on which beliefs prevail among individuals, merit and privilege can either help to perpetuate or to reject the justification of inequalities in education. This “black box” containing meritocratic beliefs and the legitimization of inequalities has only just been opened; the interaction with political identification presents another finding that may yield new insights into the value systems that characterize the political spectrums of contemporary Chile.
