Figura 4.1 shows the distribution of responses on market justice in education. Rejection clearly predominates: 78% of respondents disagree with the statement (38% strongly disagree and 40% disagree), whereas only 22% agree (16% agree and 6% strongly agree). Responses are evenly split between the two disagreement categories, while strong agreement is marginal. Although this suggests that the idea that access to better education should depend on the ability to pay has little legitimacy, we must not overlook the fact that more than one-fifth of the sample supports the commodification of education, which could have significant consequences for the legitimization of educational inequality.

Figura 4.3 presents the standardized estimates of the structural equation model, which includes age, education, sex, income quintiles and political orientation as control variables (omitted from the figure for clarity). The model fits the data well (scaled CFI = .959, TLI = .985, RMSEA = .046, 90% CI [.043, .049], SRMR = .037). Also, the measurement model is satisfactory: all standardized loadings are significant and range from .55 to .92, with the privilege factors being particularly well defined (.81 to .90). Regarding the structural paths, preferences for privilege factor show the strongest association with market justice in education (\(\beta\) = .32, p < .001), indicating that individuals who favor non-meritocratic criteria are more likely to justify a market-based education system. In contrast, perceptions of privilege factors are negatively associated with market justice (\(\beta\) = −.20, p < .001): those who perceive that parental wealth and contacts shape opportunities in Chile are less likely to endorse a market-based education. Perceived merit has a very small positive effect (\(\beta\) = .05, p < .05), while preferred merit is not significantly related to the outcome (\(\beta\) = .04, p = .294). Overall, market justice in education is shaped mainly by beliefs about privilege criteria rather than by meritocratic ones, and perceptions and preferences operate in opposite directions in meritocratic dimension.

4.1.3 Latent class analysis

We estimated latent class models with one to five classes. Model fit improved monotonically as the number of classes increased, as indicated by decreasing AIC and BIC values and statistically significant likelihood-ratio tests at each step (Tabla 4.1).

N_used <- nrow(db_lca)

m_patterns <- n_patterns_from_data(db_lca)  # scalar

# Robust extractors (avoid list-columns)
get_K <- function(fit) as.integer(ncol(fit$posterior))  # classes = cols of posterior
get_ll  <- function(fit) as.numeric(fit$llik)
get_aic <- function(fit) as.numeric(fit$aic)
get_bic <- function(fit) as.numeric(fit$bic)
get_npar <- function(fit) {
  if (!is.null(fit$npar)) return(as.numeric(fit$npar))
  # fallback: compute from probs
  K <- get_K(fit)
  Rj <- vapply(fit$probs, function(m) ncol(m), integer(1))
  as.numeric(sum(K * (Rj - 1)) + (K - 1))
}

# Table of fit indices
fit_tbl <- tibble(
  K     = map_int(fits, get_K),
  N     = nrow(db_lca),
  logLik= map_dbl(fits, get_ll),
  npar  = map_dbl(fits, get_npar),
  AIC   = map_dbl(fits, get_aic),
  BIC   = map_dbl(fits, get_bic)
) %>%
  arrange(K) %>%
  mutate(
    # Differences relative to preceding (K-1) model
    dAIC = AIC - lag(AIC),
    dBIC = BIC - lag(BIC),
    
    # Likelihood-ratio test (K vs K-1)
    # Test statistic: 2*(LL_K - LL_{K-1}) ~ Chi-square with df = npar_K - npar_{K-1}
    # Note: in LCA, models are not strictly nested in the regular sense;
    # this LRT is widely used as a heuristic, but interpret with caution.
    LRT = 2 * (logLik - lag(logLik)),
    df_LRT = npar - lag(npar),
    p_LRT = ifelse(!is.na(LRT) & df_LRT > 0, pchisq(LRT, df = df_LRT, lower.tail = FALSE), NA_real_)
  )

We can see in Tabla 4.1 that the incremental improvement in fit became substantially smaller after the four-class solution. Whereas the transition from one to four classes yielded large reductions in AIC and BIC, the five-class model provided only a modest additional improvement (AIC = 19,419.9; BIC = 19,678.4) relative to the four-class solution (AIC = 19,560.0; BIC = 19,765.6). I therefore retained the four-class solution as the final model. This choice was guided by both statistical and substantive criteria: the four-class model achieved a strong balance between goodness of fit, parsimony, and interpretability, whereas the five-class solution did not yield enough additional differentiation to justify the increase in complexity.

Class 1: Critical meritocrats (37.1%)

The first class combines low perceived meritocracy with very high perceived non-meritocracy. Respondents in this group do not believe that effort and talent are what society actually rewards, but they strongly believe that wealthy parents and social contacts shape success. Normatively, however, they strongly support meritocratic rewards and sharply reject the legitimacy of non-meritocratic advantages, especially those associated with personal networks.

This class is particularly important substantively because it captures a configuration in which meritocracy is upheld as a moral ideal while privilege is recognized as an empirical reality. In this sense, respondents in this group clearly distinguish between how society works and how it should work. They do not perceive the current system as meritocratic, but they do express strong support for a society in which effort and talent, rather than inherited or relational advantages, determine success. This class illustrates the analytical value of distinguishing perceptions from preferences, since older one-dimensional approaches to meritocratic beliefs would likely obscure this tension between normative commitment to merit and critical awareness of privilege.

Class 2: Privilege advocates (37.2%)

The second class is characterized by low perceived meritocracy and very high perceived non-meritocracy. Respondents in this group do not believe that effort or talent are strongly rewarded, but they do strongly believe that family wealth and social contacts shape life chances. At the same time, they express high support for rewarding effort and talent, yet also show high normative acceptance of advantages linked to wealthy parents and personal networks.

Substantively, this class combines a combines a realistic assessment of a stratified society with a normative justification of privilege. Thus, meritocracy is not presented as an alternative to privilege; rather, the two principles coexist and are viewed as compatible foundations for the functioning of society. This suggests a dual normative orientation in which merit and privilege are not seen as contradictory principles, but as compatible bases of social inequality.

Class 3: Universal rewards (19.0%)

The third class displays very high levels of both perceived meritocracy and perceived non-meritocracy. Respondents in this group strongly believe that effort and talent are rewarded, while also recognizing the importance of wealthy parents and social contacts. Normatively, they express very strong support for meritocratic rewards, but only moderate acceptance of non-meritocratic advantages.

This profile suggests a belief system in which merit and privilege are understood as operating simultaneously. These respondents do not deny the role of structural advantage, yet neither do they reject the view that effort and talent matter. Normatively, they strongly endorse meritocracy, while showing a more ambivalent stance toward privilege. This class can therefore be interpreted as one that sees the system as at least partially meritocratic and broadly considers this arrangement acceptable.

Class 4: Against privilege (6.7%)

The fourth class is the smallest and least clearly structured. It is characterized by a low perception of meritocracy and a low perception of the influence of privilege, specially the rich parents item. The most significant aspect of this group is that it does not perceive privilege as a factor in the distribution of resources in society, nor does it believe that it should be.

Tabla 4.2 reports four ordinal logistic regressions of market justice in education. Model 1 includes only latent class membership, Model 2 only the sociodemographic and political variables (sex, university education, income quintile and political identification), Model 3 both sets of predictors, Model 4 adds the interaction between latent class and university education to the full model, Model 5 the interaction between latent class and sex, and Model 6 the interaction between latent class and political identification. In Model 1, members of the other three classes have significantly higher odds of endorsing market justice in education than the Critical meritocrats (reference): Privilege advocates (OR = 2.50, 95% CI [2.10, 2.97]), Universal rewards (OR = 2.32, [1.88, 2.86]) and Against privilege (OR = 2.55, [1.85, 3.53]). The three coefficients are similar and their confidence intervals overlap, so the main contrast is between the Critical meritocrats and the remaining classes rather than among the latter. Model 2 shows that women (OR = 0.66) and people with university education (OR = 0.84) have lower odds of market justice, while those identifying with the center (OR = 2.03), the right (OR = 2.98) or not identifying with any position (OR = 1.75) have higher odds than those on the left; income quintiles are not significantly associated with the outcome. In the full model (Model 3), the class coefficients decrease moderately (ORs between 2.04 and 2.28) but remain significant, and the coefficients of the remaining variables change little, with the exception of political identification, whose odds ratios for the center and the right fall from 2.03 to 1.78 and from 2.98 to 2.63. This indicates that the association between latent classes and market justice is not explained by sociodemographic characteristics, although part of it overlaps with political orientation, and that the classes capture belief configurations that are related to, but distinct from, ideological position. Model 3 also has the best fit (AIC = 5,687.9 versus 5,798.0 and 5,777.0 for Models 1 and 2; BIC = 5,775.2 versus 5,832.9 and 5,846.8), so both sets of predictors contribute independently to explaining market justice in education. Finally, Model 4 shows no evidence that the association between latent classes and market justice varies by educational level: none of the interactions between class membership and university education were statistically significant (Privilege advocates OR = 1.03, [0.72, 1.48]; Universal rewards OR = 0.94, [0.61, 1.44]; Against privilege OR = 0.94, [0.47, 1.89]), and the model fits worse than Model 3 (AIC = 5,693.7; BIC = 5,798.4). Models 5 and 6 lead to the same conclusion for sex and political identification. In Model 5 none of the class-by-sex interactions is significant (Privilege advocates OR = 0.91, [0.64, 1.28]; Universal rewards OR = 0.86, [0.56, 1.31]; Against privilege OR = 0.70, [0.36, 1.35]), and in Model 6 none of the nine class-by-political-identification interactions reaches conventional significance either, the closest being Against privilege x Right (OR = 0.39, [0.15, 1.06]). # --- helpers -------------------------------------------------------------

get_K <- function(fit) {
  # robust: number of classes = columns of posterior
  if (!is.null(fit$posterior)) return(as.integer(ncol(fit$posterior)))
  # fallback: rows of probs for first item
  if (!is.null(fit$probs) && length(fit$probs) > 0) return(as.integer(nrow(fit$probs[[1]])))
  stop("Cannot determine K from poLCA object (no posterior/probs).")
}

get_npar <- function(fit) {
  if (!is.null(fit$npar)) return(as.numeric(fit$npar))
  # fallback if npar missing (rare): compute from probs + class shares
  K <- get_K(fit)
  Rj <- vapply(fit$probs, function(m) if (is.matrix(m)) ncol(m) else NA_integer_, integer(1))
  if (anyNA(Rj)) stop("Cannot compute npar: missing category counts in fit$probs.")
  as.numeric(sum(K * (Rj - 1)) + (K - 1))
}

get_n_patterns_obs <- function(fit) {
  # poLCA usually stores predicted cell counts for observed patterns as a vector
  if (!is.null(fit$predcell)) return(as.integer(length(fit$predcell)))
  # fallback: sometimes observed pattern table exists
  if (!is.null(fit$observed) && is.matrix(fit$observed)) return(as.integer(nrow(fit$observed)))
  NA_integer_
}

n_patterns_from_data <- function(data_indicators) {
  # data_indicators = db_lca with only indicators (your a1..a8), already NA-omitted
  as.integer(nrow(dplyr::distinct(data_indicators)))
}


entropy_stats <- function(fit) {
  P <- fit$posterior
  P <- pmax(P, 1e-12)
  N <- nrow(P); K <- ncol(P)
  
  # relative entropy (0..1): 1 = best separation
  rel_entropy <- 1 - (-sum(P * log(P)) / (N * log(K)))
  
  class_hat <- max.col(P)
  max_p <- apply(P, 1, max)
  
  avg_diag <- vapply(1:K, function(k) {
    idx <- which(class_hat == k)
    if (length(idx) == 0) NA_real_ else mean(P[idx, k])
  }, numeric(1))
  
  tibble(
    entropy = rel_entropy,
    mean_max_p = mean(max_p),
    p10_max_p = unname(quantile(max_p, 0.10)),
    avg_diag_min = suppressWarnings(min(avg_diag, na.rm = TRUE)),
    avg_diag_mean = mean(avg_diag, na.rm = TRUE)
  )
}

min_class_share <- function(fit) {
  Ppost <- fit$posterior
  N <- nrow(Ppost); K <- ncol(Ppost)
  class_hat <- max.col(Ppost)
  
  tibble(
    min_class_prior = min(fit$P),
    min_class_map   = min(tabulate(class_hat, nbins = K)) / N
  )
}

Model 1 includes only latent class membership, Model 2 only the sociodemographic and political variables (sex, university education, income quintile and political identification), Model 3 both sets of predictors, Model 4 adds the interaction between latent class and university education to the full model, Model 5 the interaction between latent class and sex, and Model 6 the interaction between latent class and political identification. In Model 1, members of the other three classes have significantly higher odds of endorsing market justice in education than the *Critical meritocrats* (reference): *Privilege advocates* (OR = 2.50, 95% CI [2.10, 2.97]), *Universal rewards* (OR = 2.32, [1.88, 2.86]) and *Against privilege* (OR = 2.55, [1.85, 3.53]). The three coefficients are similar and their confidence intervals overlap, so the main contrast is between the *Critical meritocrats* and the remaining classes rather than among the latter. Model 2 shows that women (OR = 0.66) and people with university education (OR = 0.84) have lower odds of market justice, while those identifying with the center (OR = 2.03), the right (OR = 2.98) or not identifying with any position (OR = 1.75) have higher odds than those on the left; income quintiles are not significantly associated with the outcome. In the full model (Model 3), the class coefficients decrease moderately (ORs between 2.04 and 2.28) but remain significant, and the coefficients of the remaining variables change little, with the exception of political identification, whose odds ratios for the center and the right fall from 2.03 to 1.78 and from 2.98 to 2.63. This indicates that the association between latent classes and market justice is not explained by sociodemographic characteristics, although part of it overlaps with political orientation, and that the classes capture belief configurations that are related to, but distinct from, ideological position. Model 3 also has the best fit (AIC = 5,687.9 versus 5,798.0 and 5,777.0 for Models 1 and 2; BIC = 5,775.2 versus 5,832.9 and 5,846.8), so both sets of predictors contribute independently to explaining market justice in education. Finally, Model 4 shows no evidence that the association between latent classes and market justice varies by educational level: none of the interactions between class membership and university education were statistically significant (*Privilege advocates* OR = 1.03, [0.72, 1.48]; *Universal rewards* OR = 0.94, [0.61, 1.44]; *Against privilege* OR = 0.94, [0.47, 1.89]), and the model fits worse than Model 3 (AIC = 5,693.7; BIC = 5,798.4). Models 5 and 6 lead to the same conclusion for sex and political identification. In Model 5 none of the class-by-sex interactions is significant (*Privilege advocates* OR = 0.91, [0.64, 1.28]; *Universal rewards* OR = 0.86, [0.56, 1.31]; *Against privilege* OR = 0.70, [0.36, 1.35]), and in Model 6 none of the nine class-by-political-identification interactions reaches conventional significance either, the closest being *Against privilege* x Right (OR = 0.39, [0.15, 1.06]). The association between latent classes and market justice in education is therefore similar across sex, educational level and political orientation.

In the BCH approach, each respondent contributes to the outcome model once per class, weighted by the inverse of the classification-error matrix of the latent class model (the diagonal of that matrix ranges from .80 to .95), and confidence intervals come from a respondent-level bootstrap with 500 replications.
