5  Results

This section is structured as follows: First, we present the descriptive results to provide an overview of how beliefs regarding market justice, as well as those regarding merit and privilege, are distributed. Second, we present the LPA, explaining how the final solution was reached and illustrating the characteristics of the individuals who make up each profile. Finally, the relationship between each of the latent profiles and market justice in education is analyzed, including the other independent variables.

5.1 Descriptives

Figure 5.1 shows the distribution of responses on market justice in education. Rejection clearly predominates: 78% of respondents disagree with the statement (38% strongly disagree and 40% disagree), whereas only 22% agree (16% agree and 6% strongly agree). Responses are evenly split between the two disagreement categories, while strong agreement is marginal. Although this suggests that the idea that access to better education should depend on the ability to pay has little legitimacy, we must not overlook the fact that more than one-fifth of the sample supports the commodification of education, which could have significant consequences for the legitimization of educational inequality.

Figure 5.2 contrasts perceptions (panel A) with preferences (panel B) about success factors, and the gap between the two is the most salient result. Perceptions are dominated by non-meritocratic factors: 94% agree that contacts and 87% that rich parents matter for getting ahead in Chile (59% and 54% strongly agree), whereas only 28% perceive that effort and 38% that talent are rewarded. Preferences show an almost mirrored pattern: 92% think effort should matter (56% strongly agree) and 73% talent, while support for rich parents (50%) and contacts (45%) falls sharply and is split almost evenly between agreement and disagreement. The gaps are therefore large and run in opposite directions: for effort, agreement rises by 64 percentage points from perceptions to preferences (28% to 92%) and for talent by 35 points (38% to 73%), while for rich parents it falls by 37 points (87% to 50%) and for contacts by 49 points (94% to 45%). In average terms (1–4 scale), preferences exceed perceptions by 1.33 points for effort and 0.73 for talent, and fall below them by 0.95 for rich parents and 1.14 for contacts (all paired Wilcoxon tests, p < .001). Overall, respondents describe an unmeritocratic society (success is perceived to depend on origin and connections) and express a clear preference for a meritocratic one, with effort as the most valued factor. Notably, a non-trivial share still endorses rich parents and contacts as legitimate determinants of success.

Figure 5.3 shows the polychoric correlations between the meritocracy indicators and market justice in education. Regarding market justice in education, the strongest associations are with the preference for parental wealth (r = .33) and for contacts (r = .33), indicating that those who consider these privilege factors legitimate are more likely to justify a market-based education. In contrast, the perception that parental wealth (r = −.19) and contacts (r = −.21) matter in Chile shows a weaker association with market justice in education in the opposite direction. The correlations with merit indicators are small (|r| ≤ .17). Among the indicators, the strongest correlations are those within each dimension: effort and talent (perceived r = .70; preferred r = .47), and parental wealth and contacts (perceived r = .74; preferred r = .72), which supports the distinction between merit and privilege dimensions. Across the results, the only moderate correlations are between perceived non-merit factors and preferred effort (.38 for parental wealth and .43 for contacts).

5.2 Latent profile analysis

Table 5.1 reports the fit indices for solutions with one to five profiles (N = 2,631). The information criteria decrease monotonically as profiles are added (BIC: 49,030 with one profile, 46,401 with three, 43,415 with five), so on their own they do not point to a clear optimum. The largest improvements occur when moving from one to two profiles (ΔBIC = −1,087) and from two to three (ΔBIC = −1,542), whereas a fourth profile yields a much smaller gain (ΔBIC = −136). The five-profile solution has the lowest information criteria and the highest entropy (.86), but its smallest profile includes only 7% of respondents. The three-profile solution shows acceptable classification quality (entropy = .72, higher than the two- and four-profile solutions, .65 and .70) and a smallest profile of 21.6% of the sample, which avoids small and unstable classes. We therefore retain the three-profile solution, as it balances fit, classification accuracy, profile size and substantive interpretability.

Figure 5.4 shows the estimated means of the eight items (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = strongly agree) for each profile of the three-profile solution. Congruent meritocrats combine the strongest endorsement of effort as a preferred success factor (3.62) with the lowest support for privilege: they prefer that parental wealth (1.65) and contacts (1.59) matter little, yet they perceive that both matter a great deal in Chile (3.79 and 3.80). Their perception of effort and talent as rewarded is the lowest of the three profiles (1.85 and 2.03), so their preferences are consistent with a merit-based ideal and critical of the current distribution of rewards. Privilege advocates share this perception of Chile (parental wealth 3.63, contacts 3.70), but their preferences differ: they endorse effort (3.57) while also accepting parental wealth (2.91) and contacts (2.81) as legitimate, the highest values on these two items. Partial meritocrats are the intermediate profile: they hold the most moderate perceptions (parental wealth 2.28, contacts 2.73) and the lowest preferences for effort (3.03) and talent (2.70), while their rejection of privilege (2.34 and 2.39) is weaker than that of Congruent meritocrats. Overall, the profiles differ little in their perceptions of effort and talent (1.85–2.36); the differences concentrate in the items on parental wealth and contacts, both in perceptions (where Partial meritocrats stand apart) and, above all, in preferences (where Privilege advocates and Congruent meritocrats are furthest apart).

5.2.1 Size of profiles

Table 5.2 shows the size of each profile according to modal assignment. Privilege advocates are the largest profile (n = 1,211; 46.0%), followed by Congruent meritocrats (n = 853; 32.4%) and Partial meritocrats (n = 567; 21.6%). The mean posterior probability of assignment is high in all three profiles (.90 for Privilege advocates and .85 for the other two), which indicates that respondents are, on average, classified with considerable certainty.

5.2.2 Bivariates of LPA

Figure 5.5, Figure 5.6 and Figure 5.7 show the composition of the latent profiles by educational level, income quintile and political identification in the regression sample (N = 2,489). Profile composition is practically the same across educational levels: Privilege advocates represent 47% of respondents without university education and 46% of those with it, Congruent meritocrats 32% and 33%, and Partial meritocrats 21% in both groups. Income shows a similarly flat pattern. The share of Congruent meritocrats ranges between 29% and 34% across quintiles, and that of Partial meritocrats between 19% and 23%, without a monotonic trend; the only visible departure is a slightly larger share of Privilege advocates in the highest quintile (50%, versus 44–47% in the other quintiles).

Political identification is where the composition of the profiles differs the most. Among respondents who identify with the left, Congruent meritocrats are the largest profile (44%) and Partial meritocrats the smallest (16%), whereas among those who identify with the right the pattern reverses: Congruent meritocrats fall to 22%, Partial meritocrats rise to 25% and Privilege advocates reach 52%. The center resembles the right (27% Congruent meritocrats, 22% Partial meritocrats and 51% Privilege advocates), and those who do not identify with any position fall in between (33%, 22% and 45%). The gap in the share of Congruent meritocrats between left and right (22 percentage points) is much larger than any difference observed by education or income, which never exceeds 6 points. Thus, the meritocratic profiles are only weakly associated with socioeconomic position, but are clearly related to political orientation, which motivates the interactions between profile and political identification estimated in the regression models.

5.3 Latent profiles and Market justice in education

5.3.1 Predicted means

Figure 5.8 shows the predicted mean of market justice in education (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = strongly agree) for each latent profile, holding sex, education, income and political identification at their observed values (specification of Model 3). Congruent meritocrats have the lowest predicted mean (1.58, 95% CI [1.53, 1.63]), clearly below Partial meritocrats (2.01, [1.94, 2.08]) and Privilege advocates (2.01, [1.96, 2.06]), whose intervals overlap almost entirely. The difference between Congruent meritocrats and the other two profiles is about 0.43 points, whereas Partial meritocrats and Privilege advocates are virtually indistinguishable. All three profiles are located below the midpoint of the scale (2.5), consistent with the general rejection of market justice in education, but only Congruent meritocrats stand out as markedly more critical.

5.3.2 Regression model

Table 5.3 reports the ordinal logistic regressions of market justice in education on the latent profiles. Congruent meritocrats are consistently less likely to endorse market justice in education than Partial meritocrats: the odds fall by 68% in the model with profiles only (OR = 0.32, 95% CI [0.26, 0.40]) and the association is virtually unchanged after adding sociodemographic controls (Model 2, OR = 0.31) and political identification (Model 3, OR = 0.35, p < .001). In contrast, Privilege advocates do not differ from Partial meritocrats in any model (OR between 0.93 and 1.00, p > .50), so the perception–preference configuration matters mainly through the meritocratic prevalence rather than through the endorsement of privilege.

Models 2 to 4 also adjust for sex (not shown in the table), and the estimates for the profiles are robust to this and to the other controls. Among the variables shown, university education is weakly and negatively associated with market justice (Model 2, OR = 0.82, p < .05; Model 3, OR = 0.84, p < .05). Income quintiles show no significant differences with respect to Q1 in any model. Political identification, introduced in Model 3, is also relevant: identifying with the left reduces the odds of supporting market justice by 38% compared to not identifying (Model 3, OR = 0.62, 95% CI [0.51, 0.76], p < .001; Model 4, OR = 0.62, p < .001), and identifying with the right increases them by 55% (Model 3, OR = 1.55, 95% CI [1.28, 1.87], p < .001); the center does not differ from the reference category (OR = 1.07 in Model 3, p > .05). Adding political identification improves model fit (AIC = 5,626 in Model 3 versus 5,694 in Model 2; BIC = 5,708 versus 5,758).

Model 4 tests whether the effect of the profiles varies among those who identify with the right. The interaction with Congruent meritocrats is positive and significant (OR = 1.71, 95% CI [1.07, 2.74], p = .026), whereas the one with Privilege advocates is not (OR = 1.26, p = .258). This means that the lower support for market justice among Congruent meritocrats is attenuated on the right: the main effect for this profile is OR = 0.30, but the combined odds ratio for right-wing Congruent meritocrats is about 0.52 (0.30 × 1.71), compared with Partial meritocrats who do not identify with the right. The interaction should be interpreted with caution, since it is only moderately significant and the interactions with the left, the center and university education (not shown in the table) were not significant.

cols <- c("Strongly disagree" = "#6a1718",

db_corr <- db %>%

# ordinal items -> polychoric correlations (with p-values, via lavaan)
fit_corr <- lavaan::lavCor(db_corr, ordered = names(db_corr),
                           estimator = "DWLS", se = "standard", output = "fit")
corr_mat <- lavaan::lavInspect(fit_corr, "cor.ov")

pe <- lavaan::parameterEstimates(fit_corr) %>%
  dplyr::filter(op == "~~", lhs != rhs)
p_mat <- matrix(0, nrow(corr_mat), ncol(corr_mat), dimnames = dimnames(corr_mat))
for (k in seq_len(nrow(pe))) {
  p_mat[pe$lhs[k], pe$rhs[k]] <- p_mat[pe$rhs[k], pe$lhs[k]] <- pe$pvalue[k]
}

corr_labels <- c(perc_effort       = "Perc. effort",

                 perc_rich_parents = "Perc. rich parents",
                 perc_contact      = "Perc. contacts",
                 pref_effort       = "Pref. effort",

                 pref_contact      = "Pref. contacts",

                           corr_labels[colnames(corr_mat)])

@tbl-polr-lpa reports the ordinal logistic regressions of market justice in education on the latent profiles. Congruent meritocrats are consistently less likely to endorse market justice in education than Partial meritocrats: the odds fall by 68% in the model with profiles only (OR = 0.32, 95% CI [0.26, 0.40]) and the association is virtually unchanged after adding sociodemographic controls (Model 2, OR = 0.31) and political identification (Model 3, OR = 0.35, *p* < .001). In contrast, Privilege advocates do not differ from Partial meritocrats in any model (OR between 0.93 and 1.00, *p* > .50), so the perception–preference configuration matters mainly through the meritocratic prevalence rather than through the endorsement of privilege.

Models 2 to 4 also adjust for sex (not shown in the table), and the estimates for the profiles are robust to this and to the other controls.                   label = factor(lab[term], levels = rev(unname(lab))),
                  type = ifelse(lo > 1 | hi < 1, "Significant (p < .05)", "Not significant"))
  ggplot(d, aes(x = or, y = label, colour = type)) +
    geom_vline(xintercept = 1, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey50") +
    geom_errorbar(aes(xmin = lo, xmax = hi), orientation = "y", width = 0.2, linewidth = 0.6) +
    geom_point(size = 2.2) +
    scale_x_log10() +
    scale_colour_manual(values = c("Significant (p < .05)" = "#B03A2E", "Not significant" = "grey50")) +
    labs(x = "Odds ratio (log scale, 95% CI)", y = NULL, colour = NULL) +
    theme_minimal() +
    theme(legend.position = "bottom")
}
