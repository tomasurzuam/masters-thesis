\chapter{Introduction}\label{introduction}

The commodification of education has been one of the most significant
transformations in the organization of welfare states over the past few
decades. Although education has traditionally been a social service
guaranteed by the state, many countries have undergone a process of
commodification that has led to a relative decline in public funding and
an expansion of private education provision
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_welfare_2020}{Busemeyer \& Iversen, 2020};
\citeproc{ref-esping-andersen_politics_1985}{Esping-Andersen, 1985};
\citeproc{ref-lindh_public_2015}{Lindh, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-oecd_education_2025}{OECD, 2025}). This shift toward
market logic has not only transformed the institutional functioning of
education systems but also people's beliefs and preferences regarding
how educational opportunities should be distributed, demonstrating the
impact of economic organization on micro-level dynamics
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_inequality_2012}{Busemeyer, 2012};
\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_investing_2018}{Busemeyer et~al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-koos_moral_2019}{Koos \& Sachweh, 2019}). In some cases,
these beliefs have led to the legitimization of educational inequality
(\citeproc{ref-castillo_changes_2025}{Castillo, Iturra, et~al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-piketty_equality_2025}{Piketty \& Sandel, 2025})

Meritocracy holds that social rewards should be distributed on the basis
of individual effort and talent, making it one of the main normative
principles associated with both education and distributional preferences
(\citeproc{ref-sandel_tyranny_2020}{Sandel, 2020}). Various studies have
shown that meritocratic beliefs are linked to higher acceptance of
inequality, as they attribute success and failure primarily to
individual responsibility, functioning as a cultural mechanism that
justifies social hierarchies and diminishes perceptions of structural
factors that shape individual trajectories
(\citeproc{ref-darnon_belief_2018}{Darnon et~al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-heuer_legitimizing_2020}{Heuer et~al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-mau_inequality_2015}{Mau, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-mijs_unfulfillable_2016}{Mijs, 2016};
\citeproc{ref-mijs_paradox_2021}{Mijs, 2021}). However, the
understanding of meritocratic beliefs has evolved in recent years, no
longer being viewed as a one-dimensional phenomenon, but rather as
capable of coexisting with other beliefs that acknowledge structural
conditions and privilege, thereby overcoming a methodological and
theoretical limitation
(\citeproc{ref-castillo_multidimensional_2023}{Castillo et~al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-zhu_meritocratic_2025}{Zhu, 2025}). Within the
multidimensional framework of meritocracy, some studies have proposed a
distinction between meritocratic perceptions and structural perceptions
(\citeproc{ref-zhu_meritocratic_2025}{Zhu, 2025}); others have focused
on descriptive and ascriptive dimensions of effort, talent, and
privilege (\citeproc{ref-castillo_multidimensional_2023}{Castillo
et~al., 2023}; \citeproc{ref-duru-bellat_whos_2012}{Duru-Bellat \&
Tenret, 2012}; \citeproc{ref-sin_they_2026}{Sin et~al., 2026}).

Several studies have examined the effect of education on the
justification of inequalities (\citeproc{ref-liu_does_2026}{Liu \& Wang,
2026}; \citeproc{ref-nye_education_2026}{Nye et~al., 2026}), or the
relationship between meritocracy and perceptions of inequality
(\citeproc{ref-bartram_does_2023}{Bartram, 2023};
\citeproc{ref-castillo_changes_2025}{Castillo, Iturra, et~al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-mijs_paradox_2021}{Mijs, 2021}), but to our knowledge, no
research has examined market preferences in education and their
relationship to meritocratic beliefs from a multidimensional and
comparative perspective. To fill this gap, this study aims to analyze
the role of meritocratic beliefs in preferences for market justice in
education and their interaction with institutional contexts. The concept
of market justice refers to the belief that access to and the quality of
social services should be determined by individual or family ability to
pay, and is thus associated with the legitimization of inequalities from
a market-oriented perspective. (\citeproc{ref-lane_market_1986}{Lane,
1986}; \citeproc{ref-lindh_public_2015}{Lindh, 2015}).

This study aims to answer three questions: (1) To what extent do
meritocratic beliefs influence preferences for market justice in
education? (2) How do institutional designs and preferences for market
justice in education interact? (3) Are there significant differences
across countries? We argue that preferences for market justice in
education are closely related to two factors: meritocratic beliefs and a
country's institutional designs. On the one hand, meritocratic beliefs
have been shown to be a determinant of distributional preferences and
perceptions of inequality, where those who perceive more meritocracy are
likely to be more inclined to prefer market justice in education {[};
Castillo et~al. (\citeproc{ref-castillo_justification_2026}{2026});
Castillo, Laffert, et~al.
(\citeproc{ref-castillo_perceptions_2025}{2025}); Castillo et~al.
(\citeproc{ref-castillo_socialization_2024}{2024}); Darnon et~al.
(\citeproc{ref-darnon_belief_2018}{2018}); Heuer et~al.
(\citeproc{ref-heuer_legitimizing_2020}{2020}); Mijs
(\citeproc{ref-mijs_paradox_2021}{2021}){]}. On the other hand,
countries' institutional designs appear to be an important factor in
shaping people's beliefs
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_education_2013}{Busemeyer, 2013};
\citeproc{ref-liu_does_2026}{Liu \& Wang, 2026}), and we hypothesize
that countries with higher levels of economic inequality, as well as
those with greater public investment in education, will exhibit an
aversion to market justice in education.

To address these issues, we use data from the 2019 International Social
Survey Program (ISSP), the World Inequality Database (WID), and UNESCO.
First, we apply latent class analysis to identify profiles of
meritocratic beliefs, and then conduct multinomial logistic regression
to examine the relationship between these profiles and preferences for
market justice in education, while incorporating sociodemographic and
institutional factors. In doing so, this research aims to contribute to
the literature on the relationship between perceptions of inequality and
meritocracy, placing special emphasis on micro-level foundations and
their interaction with institutional contexts to understand how beliefs
about educational inequality are formed.

\section{Market justice preferences}\label{market-justice-preferences}

Welfare states have undergone several institutional transformations in
recent years, with private expansion strongly penetrating the field of
social services alongside public provision
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_welfare_2020}{Busemeyer \& Iversen, 2020}).
Such processes have led to changes in the architecture of the
institutions responsible for social welfare. On the one hand, private
companies operate as service administrators of benefits based on the
user's ability to pay, whereas on the other hand, public providers have
reorganized to compete as quasi-markets
(\citeproc{ref-lindh_it_2017}{Lindh, 2017}). Besides changes at the
formal level, the commodification of social services shapes cultural
norms and beliefs, where markets operate as moral projects
(\citeproc{ref-fourcade_moral_2007}{Fourcade \& Healy, 2007};
\citeproc{ref-koos_moral_2019}{Koos \& Sachweh, 2019}).

The field of distributive justice has gradually incorporated market
preferences into attitudinal research on redistribution. Since Kluegel's
seminal work (\citeproc{ref-kluegel_beliefs_1981}{1981}) on the
normative foundations of beliefs about social stratification, the
concept of market justice preferences has been developed, understood as
the normative principle that legitimizes access to goods and services
according to an individual's or family's ability to pay
(\citeproc{ref-lane_market_1986}{Lane, 1986}). This principle is closely
linked to the justification of inequalities: by conceiving the market as
a space of equal opportunities, economic success is interpreted as an
individual achievement (\citeproc{ref-kluegel_legitimation_1999}{J. R.
Kluegel et~al., 1999}), disregarding the structural conditions that
produce such disparities. In this way, market justice operates
simultaneously as an economic and moral principle that legitimizes
socioeconomic inequalities.

Most research in this field relies on survey data, in which questions
about market justice directly probe support for the provision of social
services---education, pensions, and health care---based on individual
ability to pay (e.g., ``Is it fair that high-income individuals receive
better education than low-income individuals?'')
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_education_2013}{Busemeyer, 2013};
\citeproc{ref-lindh_public_2015}{Lindh, 2015}). Within this framework,
ISSP has been the primary source for advancing this agenda. Also, recent
research has employed both indices constructed from relevant items
(\citeproc{ref-castillo_perceptions_2025}{Castillo, Laffert, et~al.,
2025}) and a separate analysis of each item.

Education is one of the key dimensions in the study of market justice,
given the commodification processes educational systems have undergone
in recent decades. The growing prominence of the private sector in
education, coupled with the implementation of market-driven logic, has
transformed the structure of educational systems, where students and
their families are viewed as consumers of a service
(\citeproc{ref-brancaleone_educational_2011}{Brancaleone \& O'Brien,
2011}; \citeproc{ref-lindh_public_2015}{Lindh, 2015}). These changes
have ethical consequences, as education loses its intrinsic value and
becomes a means of generating monetary value, and its quality is often
determined by the type of funding, thereby reinforcing socioeconomic
inequalities (\citeproc{ref-abowitz_public_2018}{Abowitz \& Stitzlein,
2018}; \citeproc{ref-ball_education_2004}{Ball, 2004};
\citeproc{ref-piketty_equality_2025}{Piketty \& Sandel, 2025};
\citeproc{ref-satz_why_2010}{Satz, 2010}). In this sense, market justice
in education conflicts with educational justice by denying equity in
quality and universal access to education.

Recent literature on market preferences has identified significant
associations at both the individual and contextual levels. At the
individual level, socioeconomic status emerges as a consistent
predictor; those in more advantaged positions tend to exhibit greater
preferences for market justice (\citeproc{ref-koos_moral_2019}{Koos \&
Sachweh, 2019}; \citeproc{ref-lindh_public_2015}{Lindh, 2015}), a
pattern reflecting a moral economy oriented toward justifying status for
those who benefit from the market order. In this regard, political
ideology shows a clear association, with right-leaning individuals
tending to favor market-oriented distributional principles
(\citeproc{ref-castillo_socialization_2024}{Castillo et~al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-lee_fairness_2024}{Lee \& Stacey, 2024}). At the
contextual level, the relative weight of private and public service
provision emerges as a structuring factor in preferences; in countries
where private investment predominates, individuals tend to internalize
the market order as a criterion of justice and, consequently, to reduce
their redistributive preferences; whereas the opposite trend is observed
where public provision prevails
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_education_2013}{Busemeyer, 2013};
\citeproc{ref-immergut_it_2020}{Immergut \& Schneider, 2020};
\citeproc{ref-lindh_public_2015}{Lindh, 2015}).

\section{Education and legitimization of
inequalities}\label{education-and-legitimization-of-inequalities}

Education is one of the core pillars of societal structure, but the role
it plays has been the subject of an unresolved debate. From a
theoretical perspective, education plays an ambivalent role: on the one
hand, it has been argued that it promotes access to opportunities, skill
development, and the reduction of social inequalities. This perspective
is supported by various mechanisms, such as human capital development
and/or the development of critical awareness of structural problems
(\citeproc{ref-becker_human_1993}{Becker, 1993};
\citeproc{ref-freire_pedagogia_2015}{Freire, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-schultz_investment_1961}{Schultz, 1961}). On the other
hand, education has been understood as a source of social inequalities,
as it reproduces individuals' living conditions, preventing
opportunities for intergenerational social mobility and, consequently,
perpetuating the stratified order. These approaches have developed
primarily through theories that draw on symbolic violence and social
reproduction as mechanisms for legitimizing inequalities
(\citeproc{ref-bourdieu_reproduccion_2019}{Bourdieu, 2019};
\citeproc{ref-bowles_schooling_2011}{Bowles \& Gintis, 2011}). Thus,
education can be understood through its enlightening role in social
life, but also as a cog in the reproduction of inequalities in society.

Recent literature provides empirical support for these two theoretical
approaches to education. For example, research by Bubak
(\citeproc{ref-bubak_perceptions_2019}{2019}) found that higher levels
of education are negatively associated with support for meritocracy,
acknowledging that structural conditions determine individual outcomes.
Along the same lines, using the 2009 ISSP survey, Zhu
(\citeproc{ref-zhu_meritocratic_2025}{2025}) found that in Finland, the
enlightening effect of education was present, unlike in the United
States and China. In contrast, the research by Diwan \& Vartanova
(\citeproc{ref-diwan_does_2020}{2020}) concluded that states
deliberately use education to shape students' political attitudes in
order to reproduce national identity. Likewise, Van Noord et~al.
(\citeproc{ref-vannoord_educationbased_2019}{2019}) used data from 31
countries to show that education is widely understood as the basis for
social stratification. However, the results from Liu \& Wang
(\citeproc{ref-liu_does_2026}{2026}) suggest that education does not
fulfill a generalized function; rather, both the enlightening and the
reproductionist roles exist but depend on the macro context, where the
enlightening role is present in countries with low levels of inequality,
while the legitimizing role is observed in countries with high levels of
inequality.

Besides to the functional role of education, a vast body of literature
has situated education within the framework of redistributive
preferences. In a cross-country study, Busemeyer et~al.
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_investing_2018}{2018}) found that education is
the policy field with the highest level of support for spending;
however, when distinguishing between types of spending, higher levels of
investment in private education are negatively associated with
preferences for redistribution
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_education_2013}{Busemeyer, 2013}). In
country-specific studies such as those in Australia and Chile, most
respondents stated that they consider it unfair for the quality of
education to depend on parents' ability to pay
(\citeproc{ref-castillo_changes_2025}{Castillo, Iturra, et~al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-lee_fairness_2024}{Lee \& Stacey, 2024}). Furthermore,
based on correlational and experimental evidence, Soler‐Martínez et~al.
(\citeproc{ref-soler-martinez_income_2025}{2025}) found that perceived
educational inequality is associated with greater intolerance of
inequality.

Surveys have been the primary tool for studying educational
inequalities, measuring the concept through individuals' perceptions. In
this frame, ISSP, as one of the most globally recognized surveys for
measuring inequality, poses the question: \emph{``Is it fair that
high-income individuals have better education than lower-income
individuals?''}. Some studies have incorporated this operationalization
into national surveys (\citeproc{ref-castillo_changes_2025}{Castillo,
Iturra, et~al., 2025}). Other studies have developed questions aimed at
measuring the perceived gap in educational inequality (\emph{i.e., ``In
your judgment, how large or small are the differences in education
between the rich and poor in Spain?''})
(\citeproc{ref-soler-martinez_income_2025}{Soler‐Martínez et~al.,
2025}). Busemeyer et~al. (\citeproc{ref-busemeyer_investing_2018}{2018})
conducted a survey in which the focus of the question was on the
distribution of education spending, rather than on the judgment of
inequality: \emph{Let's talk about the distribution of public spending
in the education sector. Please tell me whether you would like to see
more or less government spending in each of the following areas. Keep in
mind that `more' or `much more' might require a tax increase}. All
operationalizations share the general objective of measuring educational
inequalities, but each employs a different strategy and focuses on a
distinct dimension of the concept.
